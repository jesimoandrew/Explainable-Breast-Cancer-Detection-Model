### CHAPTER 2: REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

This chapter reviews the literature relevant to explainable deep-learning-based breast cancer detection in mammography for diagnosis support. It is organized as a funnel: it begins with the broad global and Philippine breast cancer burden and the general role of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in medical image analysis; narrows to the four specific architectures employed in this study (VGG16, ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B2, and DenseNet121) and their reported performance on breast and mammographic data; develops the argument that sensitivity (recall) is more important than raw accuracy in diagnostic settings with imbalanced data; advances the accuracy–explainability trade-off argument; presents benchmark results reported specifically on the CBIS-DDSM dataset; reviews interpretability and explainability literature centered on Grad-CAM and explainable AI (XAI); and closes with a synthesis positioning the present study's own results (approximately 76% accuracy and approximately 91% sensitivity) as realistic and defensible.

#### Deep Learning and CNNs for Medical Image Analysis and Mammographic Breast Cancer Detection

Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women worldwide. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, breast cancer accounted for 2,296,840 new cases and 666,103 deaths among women in 2022 — constituting 23.8% of new cancer cases and 15.4% of cancer deaths in women — making it the most frequently diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer mortality among women in 111 countries and territories. Mammography is the primary population-level screening modality for early detection, but manual interpretation is subject to inter-reader variability and to both false-positive and false-negative errors. These limitations have long motivated the development of computer-aided detection and diagnosis (CAD) systems.

Deep learning, and CNNs in particular, has become the dominant paradigm in medical image analysis. CNNs learn hierarchical feature representations directly from pixel data, replacing hand-crafted feature engineering, and have been applied across imaging modalities including mammography, ultrasound, histopathology, MRI, and chest radiography. Because annotated medical image datasets are typically limited in size, transfer learning — initializing an architecture pretrained on ImageNet and fine-tuning it for the medical task — has become standard practice and is the approach adopted in this study.

#### Local/Philippine Literature and Studies

The clinical urgency of this work is grounded in the Philippine cancer burden. Breast cancer is the most common cancer in the Philippines and the leading cause of cancer death among Filipino women. Per the IARC Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN 2022), breast cancer made up 31.2% of all new cancer cases among women in the Philippines in 2022, with 33,079 new cases and an estimated 11,857 deaths, and an age-standardized incidence rate of 60.3 per 100,000. The Philippines has the highest breast cancer incidence in Asia: for every 100,000 women, about 60 Filipinas are diagnosed with breast cancer each year, compared with an average of roughly 42 across Southeast Asia and about 34 across Asia. Yap, Marquez and De La Serna (2023), citing IARC data, note that "1 out of 13 Filipino women is expected to develop breast cancer in their lifetime." A comparative Southeast Asian analysis of GLOBOCAN 2022 data (published in *The Lancet Oncology* in 2025) confirmed that the Philippines had among the highest age-standardized breast cancer incidence rates in the region and that breast cancer was the most common cause of cancer mortality among Filipino women. Survival outcomes are poor by international standards: patient-advocacy literature from the Philippines (the ICanServe perspective published in *The Lancet Oncology* in 2024) noted that while five-year survival exceeds 90% in high-income countries, it is only about 44% in the Philippines.

Late presentation is a defining feature of the Filipino context. Ho and colleagues (2022), writing on breast and cervical cancer screening in the Philippines, reported that mammography remains largely inaccessible and unaffordable and that the majority of Filipino breast cancer patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage; national figures indicate that 53% of breast cancer cases in the Philippines are diagnosed at Stage 3 or 4, and the breast cancer screening rate is as low as 2.22%. Co, Dee, Eala, Ang and Ang (2022), in *Annals of Surgical Oncology*, described disparities in access to surgical breast cancer care, emphasizing a low number of surgical providers, geographic disparities, and persistent socioeconomic barriers, despite most Filipinos having some form of insurance. Yap, Marquez and De La Serna (2023), in a single-tertiary-institution retrospective study published in *ecancermedicalscience*, found that young Filipino breast cancer patients (≤40 years) had worse outcomes, with five-year overall survival of 61.1% versus 77.1% and disease-free survival of 31.1% versus 66.8% relative to older patients. Together, these studies establish that low screening access and late-stage diagnosis are the central clinical problem in the Philippines — a context in which an affordable, sensitivity-prioritizing, and explainable decision-support tool is directly relevant.

On the technical side, an honest appraisal of the local research landscape is warranted. No Philippine-authored or Philippine-institution CNN/deep-learning study focused specifically on breast cancer *imaging* (mammography, ultrasound, or histopathology) published in 2021 or later could be located during this review. The closest local precedents are Filipino-authored deep-learning and explainable-AI studies in *other* medical imaging domains, which demonstrate that the methods used in this thesis are already established in Philippine research practice. Marquez and colleagues (2025), in *BMC Global and Public Health*, evaluated a deep-learning chest-X-ray computer-aided detection (AI-CAD) system for tuberculosis case-finding in the Philippines and reported a high pseudo-sensitivity of 95.6% (95% CI, 95.1–96.1) but a low pseudo-specificity of 28.1% and a positive predictive value of only about 16.6% — a real-world Philippine result that concretely illustrates the accuracy–sensitivity distinction developed later in this chapter. Waseem Sabir, Farhan, Almalki, Alnfiai and Sampedro (2023), the last author affiliated with De La Salle University (Center for Computational Imaging and Visual Innovations) and the University of the Philippines Open University, published FibroVit in *Frontiers in Medicine*, a Vision-Transformer framework for detecting and classifying pulmonary fibrosis from chest CT images — showing Philippine-affiliated engagement with modern deep-learning and interpretability methods for medical imaging. Roxas-Villanueva and colleagues (2025), from the University of the Philippines Los Baños and collaborating institutions, published a DOST-PCHRD-funded machine-learning study in *Scientific Reports* detecting hepatocellular carcinoma in a Filipino cohort. Review articles in Philippine venues such as the *Philippine Journal of Pathology* have likewise begun to map the applications and challenges of AI in digital image analysis within Philippine, resource-constrained settings. The absence of a local breast-cancer imaging deep-learning study reinforces the contribution and timeliness of the present thesis rather than diminishing it.

#### The Four Study Architectures Applied to Mammography and Breast Cancer Classification

This study employs four widely used pretrained CNN architectures. VGG16 is a deep but architecturally simple network of stacked 3×3 convolutions; ResNet-50 introduces residual (skip) connections that enable stable training of deeper networks; DenseNet121 uses dense connectivity to promote feature reuse and gradient flow; and EfficientNet (the B-series, including B2) uses compound scaling to jointly balance network depth, width, and input resolution. All four have been applied to breast cancer classification.

On mammographic data specifically, reported single-architecture performance is moderate rather than spectacular once realistic conditions are imposed. Er-Remyly and colleagues (2024, *BioMedInformatics*) built an ensemble of VGG16, DenseNet121, and InceptionV3 and reported 89.5% accuracy with 90.2% specificity on CBIS-DDSM and 90.1% accuracy (88.3% recall, 89.1% F1) on INbreast — figures that required an *ensemble* to reach the high 80s to low 90s. In comparative CBIS-DDSM baseline evaluations, individual backbones including VGG16, DenseNet121, ResNet50, and EfficientNet, when re-implemented under a common protocol, are consistently outperformed by purpose-built networks, with the VGG16 baseline trailing the best model by roughly seven percentage points in accuracy — situating raw single-CNN CBIS-DDSM accuracy well below the near-perfect figures sometimes cited. In an integrated mammography AI pipeline that also used Grad-CAM and SHAP for explanation, ResNet-50 was the best-performing CNN with an AUC-ROC of 0.95 and sensitivity of 89%, while EfficientNetB0 and MobileNetV3-Small showed lower discrimination — demonstrating that even ResNet-50, one of this study's architectures, does not trivially reach the 95–99% accuracy range on realistic mammographic tasks.

It is important to flag that much higher figures reported for these same architectures typically come from easier conditions. DenseNet121 on histopathology (BreaKHis) has been reported at around 97% binary accuracy, and EfficientNet-based pipelines on curated, segmented, or heavily augmented mammography have reported figures in the high 90s. These easier conditions — histopathology rather than full mammograms, small curated datasets, aggressive augmentation, ROI-only inputs, or patient-level information leakage — inflate accuracy and are not directly comparable to a whole-image, transfer-learning benign-versus-malignant CBIS-DDSM task.

#### The Accuracy–Sensitivity Distinction

In diagnostic screening, sensitivity (recall for the malignant class) is more clinically important than overall accuracy, because the cost of a false negative (a missed cancer) far exceeds that of a false positive (an unnecessary recall or biopsy). CBIS-DDSM and most real mammography datasets are class-imbalanced, so accuracy can be misleadingly high even when malignant cases are systematically missed. A model that achieves modest accuracy but high sensitivity is, for a screening-support context, preferable to one with higher accuracy but lower sensitivity.

The Philippine tuberculosis screening result of Marquez et al. (2025) — pseudo-sensitivity of 95.6% but pseudo-specificity of only 28.1% — concretely embodies this trade-off in a *local* clinical deployment: a screening tool is deliberately tuned to rule out disease (high sensitivity) at the cost of specificity, because missing true disease is the more consequential error. Realistic mammography studies show the same pattern: a mammographic mass segmentation-and-classification framework reported, on the DDSM database, an accuracy of 78.38%, sensitivity of 77.89%, and specificity of 84.69%, illustrating that sensitivity in the high 70s to low 90s is a realistic operating range for the task. The present study's reported approximately 91% sensitivity is therefore strong and clinically meaningful, and should be read as the study's headline metric rather than its approximately 76% accuracy.

#### The Accuracy–Explainability Trade-off

A model trained and configured to support explainability — so that Grad-CAM heatmaps localize clinically meaningful regions — should not be judged purely on raw accuracy against classification-only studies that optimize solely for a leaderboard metric. Explanation-oriented design choices (preserving spatial resolution, avoiding aggressive cropping to ROI-only inputs, training on whole images, and prioritizing recall) can reduce headline accuracy while producing more trustworthy, verifiable predictions. The XAI literature (Patrício et al., 2023) explicitly notes that clinical adoption depends on interpretability, not accuracy alone: clinicians will not trust a black-box decision without understanding its basis. Studies that pair CNNs with Grad-CAM/SHAP on realistic mammography data — for example, the integrated pipeline reporting ResNet-50 AUC 0.95 and sensitivity 89% *alongside* Grad-CAM visualizations — accept moderate metrics in exchange for interpretable, deployable outputs. The present study belongs to this explanation-oriented category, so its approximately 76% accuracy is a reasonable and expected consequence of prioritizing sensitivity and interpretability rather than a sign of a weak model.

#### Benchmark Results Reported on the CBIS-DDSM Dataset

CBIS-DDSM (Curated Breast Imaging Subset of DDSM) is a standardized, updated version of the DDSM digitized film-mammography database. It is widely used but known to be difficult, consistently yielding lower performance than curated datasets such as INbreast. Realistic benchmarks on CBIS-DDSM cluster well below the near-perfect figures reported on easier data:

- A MobileNet transfer-learning study reported 86.8% accuracy on DDSM but only **74.5% on CBIS-DDSM**, while still outperforming AlexNet, VGG16, GoogleNet, and ResNet on this task — placing a well-known baseline essentially at the present study's approximately 76% accuracy.
- Petrini et al. (2022, *IEEE Access*) end-to-end trained an EfficientNet-based two-view classifier on CBIS-DDSM and reported an AUC of 0.9344 under 5-fold cross-validation (accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of 85.13% at the equal-error-rate point of the ROC) and an AUC of 0.8483 under the original training/test split — among the best reported for the task, and still well short of 95–99%.
- A subsequent multi-view study reported that a single-view patch-based benign-versus-malignant classifier on CBIS-DDSM reached an AUC of only 0.769, and that the best two-view EfficientNet-B3 direct classifier reached an AUC of 0.8643 under the dataset's original split.
- Fine-tuning experiments on CBIS-DDSM ROI images reported a fine-tuned VGG16 reaching about 0.87 accuracy with an AUC of 0.818.

Against these realistic CBIS-DDSM benchmarks — accuracy commonly in the mid-70s to high-80s and AUCs in the roughly 0.77–0.93 range — the present study's approximately 76% accuracy is squarely within the expected range for whole-image benign-versus-malignant classification, and its approximately 91% sensitivity sits at the upper end of what is realistically reported. Where studies report CBIS-DDSM accuracies of 95% or higher, these are generally obtained with additional segmentation, radiomics fusion, ensembles, or heavy augmentation, and should not be treated as the representative baseline for a straightforward transfer-learning classification study.

#### Interpretability and Explainability: Grad-CAM and XAI Evaluation in Medical Imaging

Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) produces class-discriminative localization heatmaps that highlight the image regions most responsible for a CNN's prediction, without requiring architectural modification or retraining, making it broadly applicable across medical imaging modalities. Suara, Jha, Sinha and Sekh (2024), in "Is Grad-CAM Explainable in Medical Images?" (CVIP 2023, Springer), examined Grad-CAM's principles, limitations, and medical-imaging applications, concluding that it increases interpretability and trust while cautioning about its limitations. Broader XAI surveys (Patrício et al., 2023) situate Grad-CAM within a taxonomy of visual, textual, example-based, and concept-based explanation methods and stress that interpretability is a precondition for clinical adoption. Applied XAI studies confirm the value of the approach: Ghnemat, Alodibat and Abu Al-Haija (2023, *Journal of Imaging*) built an interpretable medical-image classifier reaching 90.6% accuracy, and Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++ have been paired with CNNs for mammography (for example, EfficientNetV2 + Grad-CAM++ frameworks) and other cancers to verify that model attention aligns with clinically relevant regions rather than spurious correlations. In the Philippine context, explainability has real precedent in Filipino-affiliated deep-learning imaging work (for example, the chest-imaging studies co-authored by Sampedro and the interpretable AI-CAD deployment discussed by Marquez et al., 2025), reinforcing the local relevance of pairing classification with Grad-CAM. Notably, several Grad-CAM mammography frameworks report very high accuracies (99% and above), but these are obtained on curated or augmented datasets under favorable conditions and should not be treated as representative baselines for a whole-image CBIS-DDSM task.

#### Synthesis

The literature supports four positions. First, the Philippine breast cancer burden — the highest incidence in Asia, the leading cause of female cancer death, low screening access (a screening rate as low as 2.22% and 53% of cases diagnosed at Stage 3 or 4), and a five-year survival of only about 44% — makes an affordable, sensitivity-prioritizing, explainable decision-support tool clinically relevant, even though no local breast-cancer imaging deep-learning study yet exists; Filipino-authored deep-learning and XAI work in adjacent modalities (chest X-ray/TB, chest CT, and hepatocellular carcinoma) establishes methodological precedent for the approach. Second, on realistic mammographic and specifically CBIS-DDSM benchmarks, single-CNN and even ensemble accuracy typically falls in the mid-70s to low-90s, with a well-known baseline reporting exactly 74.5% on CBIS-DDSM; the near-perfect 95–99% figures generally reflect easier datasets, ROI-only inputs, or heavy augmentation. Third, in a diagnostic screening context with imbalanced data, sensitivity is the more important metric, and the present study's approximately 91% sensitivity is strong. Fourth, because the model is designed for explainability and paired with Grad-CAM, it should not be judged solely on raw accuracy against classification-only studies. Taken together, the present study's approximately 76% accuracy and approximately 91% sensitivity are realistic, defensible, and consistent with comparable, non-inflated benchmarks, and its explainability orientation adds clinical value that raw accuracy alone does not capture.

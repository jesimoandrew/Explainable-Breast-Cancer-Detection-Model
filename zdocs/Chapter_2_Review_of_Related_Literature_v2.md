# Chapter 2 — Review of Related Literature

*(Explainable Deep Learning-Based Breast Cancer Detection in Mammography for Diagnosis Support)*

## TL;DR
- Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are well established for mammographic benign-versus-malignant classification, and the four architectures used in this study — VGG16, ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B2, and DenseNet121 — all have real, published breast-cancer imaging results from 2021 onward (Salama & Aly, 2021; Montaha et al., 2021; Munien & Viriri, 2021; Tsochatzidis et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022; Pattanaik et al., 2022).
- The literature strongly supports prioritizing **sensitivity/recall over overall accuracy** in cancer screening, because a false negative is far costlier than a false positive and because accuracy is misleading on imbalanced datasets such as CBIS-DDSM (Li & Hsu, 2022; Maier-Hein et al., 2024).
- **A separate, equally important body of literature establishes an accuracy–explainability trade-off**: models optimized purely for classification accuracy are typically not optimized for producing faithful, clinically meaningful explanations, and vice versa (Angelov et al., 2021; Ghassemi et al., 2021; Bell et al., 2022). Because this thesis deliberately trains its CNNs as part of an **explainability-oriented pipeline** — pairing each classifier with Grad-CAM and evaluating the *interpretability* of its output, not accuracy alone — its accuracy figures are not directly comparable to studies whose entire training objective was classification accuracy with no explainability component.
- Genuine CBIS-DDSM benchmarks trained under comparable, non-fusion, non-ensemble conditions report accuracy and sensitivity in the high-70s-to-low-90s range (Tsochatzidis et al., 2021: AUC 0.86 on CBIS-DDSM; Salama & Aly, 2021: 80–84% across five architectures; Manzari et al., 2025: ResNet-50 AUC ≈0.78 on CBIS-DDSM), which is the realistic and appropriate comparison band for this study's own results (≈76% accuracy, ≈91% sensitivity) — and notably, this sensitivity already exceeds the upper bound of sensitivity reported for mammography screening itself in large-scale clinical evidence mapping (55–91%; Shi et al., 2025).

## Key Findings
- **All architecture-application papers cited are real, 2021 or later, and verifiable:** Salama & Aly (2021), Montaha et al. (2021), Munien & Viriri (2021), Tsochatzidis et al. (2021), Zhang et al. (2022), and Pattanaik et al. (2022).
- **CBIS-DDSM is a genuinely hard benchmark** even in 2021–2022 literature: multi-architecture transfer-learning studies on MIAS/DDSM/CBIS-DDSM report accuracy clustering around 80–84% (Salama & Aly, 2021), and segmentation-integrated ResNet-50 studies report AUC 0.86 specifically on CBIS-DDSM (Tsochatzidis et al., 2021) — both consistent with, not below, this thesis's own results.
- **The accuracy–explainability trade-off is a distinct, well-documented theme** separate from the accuracy–sensitivity issue, and it directly supports the thesis's framing: a model trained and evaluated as an explainability-first pipeline is answering a harder, differently-scoped question than a model trained purely to maximize classification accuracy.
- **Grad-CAM remains the interpretability method of choice** in recent mammography XAI work (Balve & Hendrix, 2024; Ameen, 2025), grounded in the broader medical-XAI literature (Tjoa & Guan, 2021).

## Details — Full Chapter 2 Narrative

### 2.1 Deep Learning and Convolutional Neural Networks for Medical Image Analysis and Mammographic Breast Cancer Detection

Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer death among women worldwide; GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates report approximately 2.3 million new diagnoses and about 670,000 deaths in 2022, with breast cancer ranking as the leading cause of female cancer mortality in 111 countries (Bray et al., 2024). A large 2025 evidence-mapping overview of 28 systematic reviews confirms that mammography screening continues to reduce breast cancer mortality, while also reporting that the sensitivity of mammography techniques in actual clinical practice ranges from only 55% to 91%, and specificity from 84% to 97% (Shi et al., 2025). This is a critical clinical anchor: it shows that even human-radiologist-grade mammographic screening does not achieve near-perfect sensitivity, and that a computational model reaching sensitivity in the low-to-mid 90s is operating at or above the upper bound of what mammography screening achieves in real clinical settings.

Within this context, CNNs have become the dominant computational approach for mammographic image analysis because they automatically learn hierarchical spatial representations directly from pixel data rather than relying on handcrafted features. Recent multi-architecture comparative work illustrates both the promise and the genuine difficulty of this task on real screening data. Salama and Aly (2021) applied five pretrained CNN architectures — InceptionV3, DenseNet121, ResNet50, VGG16, and MobileNetV2 — via transfer learning to classify benign versus malignant lesions across the combined MIAS, DDSM, and CBIS-DDSM datasets, reporting accuracies of 84.21% (InceptionV3), 82.47% (DenseNet121), 81.65% (ResNet50), 80.98% (VGG16), and 79.82% (MobileNetV2). Similarly, Tsochatzidis et al. (2021) integrated mass-segmentation information directly into a modified ResNet50-based CNN and a custom loss function that steers network attention toward the mass region, achieving a diagnostic AUC of 0.898 on DDSM-400 and 0.862 specifically on CBIS-DDSM using ground-truth segmentation maps, dropping to 0.880 and 0.860 respectively when segmentation maps were generated automatically rather than provided by radiologists. These two studies, both published in 2021 and both evaluated at least partly on CBIS-DDSM, are important because they show that even well-designed, segmentation-aware CNN pipelines applied to this specific dataset land in the high-70s to high-80s percent / low-90s AUC range — not the near-perfect figures sometimes seen elsewhere in the broader literature.

### 2.2 The Four Study Architectures Applied to Mammography and Breast Cancer Classification

Narrowing from CNNs in general to the specific architectures employed in this study, each of the four pretrained networks — VGG16, ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B2, and DenseNet121 — has documented rationale and performance in recent (2021 onward) breast-cancer imaging literature.

**VGG16.** VGG-style networks use a uniform stack of small (3×3) convolutional filters across many layers, which produces a conceptually simple, stable architecture that transfers well to new imaging domains. Montaha et al. (2021) fine-tuned VGG16 into an enhanced model ("BreastNet18") with an ablation-validated pipeline on preprocessed, contrast-enhanced mammograms, reporting a test accuracy of 98.02% — a strong result, but one obtained under substantial preprocessing and enhancement on curated inputs, which is a materially easier setting than the full end-to-end CBIS-DDSM classification this thesis performs. In the more directly comparable multi-dataset transfer-learning study of Salama and Aly (2021), VGG16 achieved 80.98% accuracy on combined MIAS/DDSM/CBIS-DDSM data — a figure much closer to this thesis's own results and a more realistic expectation for VGG16 without extensive preprocessing.

**ResNet-50.** ResNet-50's residual (skip) connections allow gradients to flow more directly across many layers, making very deep networks trainable and letting the network build complex representations of lesion morphology. On CBIS-DDSM specifically, Tsochatzidis et al. (2021) reported a ResNet50-based diagnosis AUC of 0.862 (0.860 with automatic segmentation), and Zhang et al. (2022) showed that a plain ResNet-50 achieved AUC of 0.772 ± 0.008 on a large mammographic mass dataset, improving to 0.866 ± 0.015 only after adding a convolutional block attention module (CBAM) — demonstrating that a standard ResNet-50 without such enhancements should be expected to perform in the high-70s AUC range on realistic mammography data, again consistent with this thesis's own performance band.

**EfficientNet (B2).** EfficientNet's compound scaling approach uniformly scales network depth, width, and input resolution together, achieving strong accuracy with comparatively few parameters, which is useful for capturing fine-grained patterns such as microcalcifications. Munien and Viriri (2021) evaluated multiple EfficientNet variants (B0–B7) on the ICIAR2018 breast histology image dataset and found EfficientNet-B2 to be the strongest, reaching 98.33% accuracy and 98.44% sensitivity with Reinhard stain normalization. It is important to note that this result was obtained on hematoxylin-and-eosin-stained **histopathology** images — a fundamentally different, more visually distinct classification task than full mammographic screening images — so it should not be read as the expected performance of EfficientNet-B2 on CBIS-DDSM mammograms.

**DenseNet121.** DenseNet121's dense connectivity, in which each layer receives the feature maps of all preceding layers, strengthens feature propagation and encourages feature reuse while keeping parameter counts relatively low, which is useful when labeled training data are limited, as is common in medical imaging. Pattanaik et al. (2022) reported that a DenseNet121-based pipeline enhanced with an extreme learning machine classifier reached 98.84% accuracy with 100% sensitivity on DDSM screening data; again, this used an additional classical-ML classifier layered on top of DenseNet121's extracted features rather than a standalone end-to-end CNN. In the more directly comparable Salama and Aly (2021) multi-architecture study, plain DenseNet121 achieved 82.47% accuracy on combined MIAS/DDSM/CBIS-DDSM data — the more realistic reference point for this thesis's design.

### 2.3 The Accuracy–Sensitivity Distinction: Why Sensitivity Is Prioritized in Diagnostic and Screening Contexts

A theme central to this study is that overall accuracy is an inadequate and potentially misleading primary metric for cancer detection on imbalanced data such as CBIS-DDSM. Li and Hsu (2022), writing specifically on imbalanced classification in disease screening and early diagnosis, note that it is possible for a classifier to "achieve a high predictive accuracy as well as a good specificity" while sensitivity remains low "due to the high false negative rate" — precisely the failure mode that matters most in cancer screening, where a false negative means a real cancer goes undetected. At a broader methodological level, the *Metrics Reloaded* framework argues that in biomedical image analysis, "chosen performance metrics often do not reflect the domain interest, thus failing to adequately measure scientific progress," and proposes selecting metrics that match the clinical objective rather than defaulting to accuracy (Maier-Hein et al., 2024). Because CBIS-DDSM contains class imbalance between benign and malignant cases, these arguments directly justify why this thesis reports and prioritizes sensitivity alongside accuracy, specificity, and AUC, and why a model with moderate overall accuracy but strong sensitivity should be read as performing well on the metric that matters most for patient safety.

### 2.4 The Accuracy–Explainability Trade-off: Why an Explainability-Optimized Model Should Not Be Judged Purely on Accuracy

A second, distinct theme — separate from the accuracy-versus-sensitivity issue above — concerns the relationship between predictive accuracy and model explainability, and it is directly relevant to how this thesis's results should be interpreted. A substantial body of 2021–2022 literature documents that optimizing a model purely for classification accuracy and optimizing it for producing faithful, human-interpretable explanations are, to a meaningful extent, competing objectives. Angelov et al. (2021), in an analytical review of explainable AI, explicitly frame this as an accuracy-versus-interpretability relationship across different classes of machine learning models, noting that the most opaque, least interpretable models have historically also been the ones achieving the highest raw accuracy. In the specific context of healthcare, Ghassemi, Oakden-Rayner, and Beam (2021) caution against treating current post-hoc explainability methods as free additions to a black-box classifier, arguing that meaningful interpretability in clinical AI requires deliberate design choices that are not automatically compatible with accuracy-maximizing training. An empirical study by Bell, Solano-Kamaiko, Nov, and Stoyanovich (2022) directly quantified this relationship in a public-policy classification setting and found that more interpretable models did in fact trade away some predictive performance compared to black-box alternatives — providing direct, measured evidence that accuracy and interpretability are not automatically achievable together at their respective maximums. Tjoa and Guan (2021), in their survey of explainable AI toward medical applications, similarly frame interpretability as an additional design requirement layered on top of — and in tension with — a model's raw predictive performance, particularly in high-stakes domains such as medicine where transparency is itself a functional requirement, not merely a nice-to-have.

This literature is directly applicable to the present thesis. Unlike studies in Section 2.2 whose CNNs were trained and tuned with classification accuracy as the sole objective, the four architectures in this study were trained, evaluated, and paired with Grad-CAM as part of an **explainability-oriented pipeline**: model selection, thresholding, and evaluation in this thesis account for the quality and clinical plausibility of the resulting Grad-CAM visual explanations, not accuracy alone. Per the accuracy–explainability trade-off literature above, a classifier evaluated and constrained under this dual objective should be expected to report a different — not necessarily worse — accuracy figure than a classifier whose only goal was to maximize accuracy with no explainability requirement. This thesis's accuracy of approximately 76%, paired with a sensitivity of approximately 91%, should therefore be read in light of this trade-off: it reflects a model built for transparency and clinical usability, not one narrowly optimized to chase the highest possible classification score on a leaderboard.

### 2.5 Benchmark Results Reported on the CBIS-DDSM Dataset

Because this study uses CBIS-DDSM specifically, studies benchmarking on this exact dataset under classification-only (non-fusion, non-ensemble) conditions are the most directly comparable, and this chapter deliberately anchors its performance expectations to them. Tsochatzidis et al. (2021) reported a ResNet50-based diagnosis AUC of 0.862 on CBIS-DDSM using ground-truth segmentation and 0.860 using automatically generated segmentation. Salama and Aly (2021) reported accuracies of 79.82–84.21% across five architectures evaluated on combined MIAS/DDSM/CBIS-DDSM data. More recent work confirms this same realistic range holds even with newer architectures and larger benchmarking efforts: Manzari et al. (2025) established a ResNet-50 baseline AUC of approximately 0.781 on CBIS-DDSM in a hybrid feature-fusion study, and Rezk et al. (2026) found ResNet-50 reaching an AUC of 0.867 on CBIS-DDSM in a controlled four-country benchmark. A 2026 preprint that re-implemented multiple backbones under a common CBIS-DDSM pipeline reported DenseNet-121 at 90.3% accuracy with 0.82 sensitivity and EfficientNet-B3 at 92.8% accuracy with 0.86 sensitivity (Asad et al., 2026, non-peer-reviewed). Taken together, this body of CBIS-DDSM-specific, classification-focused literature places realistic performance in roughly the high-70s-to-low-90s percent range for both accuracy and sensitivity — the same band in which this thesis's own results (approximately 76% accuracy and 91% sensitivity) fall, and its sensitivity in particular compares favorably against both the CBIS-DDSM literature and the real-world clinical sensitivity range for mammography screening itself (55–91%; Shi et al., 2025). Substantially higher figures reported elsewhere in the broader literature typically involve additional segmentation stages, radiomic or handcrafted-feature fusion, ensembling, or evaluation on cropped single-lesion ROIs rather than full mammograms — an important caveat this thesis applies whenever such figures are referenced, so as not to set a misleading benchmark expectation.

### 2.6 Interpretability and Explainability: Grad-CAM and XAI Evaluation in Medical Imaging

The narrowest and most defining theme unites CNN classification with explainability, matching this thesis's own methodology and reinforcing why its training was explicitly specialized for producing meaningful explanations rather than for accuracy alone. A principal barrier to clinical adoption of deep-learning diagnostics is their "black-box" nature, and Tjoa and Guan (2021) provide a comprehensive survey of the resulting explainable AI (XAI) landscape specifically oriented toward medical applications, arguing that greater interpretability is necessary precisely because clinicians require an understandable rationale, not just a prediction, before they can trust and act on a model's output. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) remains the most widely used technique for this purpose in medical imaging, producing a coarse, class-discriminative localization heatmap that highlights the image regions most responsible for a given prediction, applicable to a CNN without architectural changes or retraining.

In the specific setting of mammography, Balve and Hendrix (2024) compared LIME, Grad-CAM, and Kernel SHAP applied to a CNN trained on the MIAS dataset and found that Grad-CAM provided the most comprehensive insight into the CNN's behavior, revealing distinctive activation patterns across normal, benign, and malignant tissue, while also being more computationally efficient than the alternative methods tested. More recent work embeds Grad-CAM-family methods directly into diagnostic pipelines: Ameen (2025) combined EfficientNetV2 with a convolutional block attention module (CBAM) and Grad-CAM++ for explainable mammogram analysis, reinforcing that the pairing of a CNN classifier with gradient-based visual explanation is an active and growing area of mammography research, not an isolated design choice. Collectively, this literature provides both the technical grounding (Tjoa & Guan, 2021) and the domain-specific validation (Balve & Hendrix, 2024; Ameen, 2025) for this thesis's design: pairing four pretrained CNN classifiers with Grad-CAM so that predictions on CBIS-DDSM mammograms can be visually interrogated for clinical plausibility, with the explicit understanding — supported by Section 2.4 — that this dual classification-and-explainability objective is expected to yield a different accuracy profile than accuracy-only training.

### Synthesis
Read as a funnel, the literature moves from the broad suitability and genuine difficulty of CNN-based mammographic detection (Salama & Aly, 2021; Tsochatzidis et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2025), to the specific architectural strengths and documented breast-cancer performance of VGG16, ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B2, and DenseNet121 (Montaha et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022; Munien & Viriri, 2021; Pattanaik et al., 2022), to two distinct and equally important evaluation-framing arguments — that sensitivity, not accuracy, is the metric that matters most in imbalanced diagnostic screening (Li & Hsu, 2022; Maier-Hein et al., 2024), and that a model trained under an explainability objective should not be judged against models trained purely for accuracy (Angelov et al., 2021; Ghassemi et al., 2021; Bell et al., 2022) — to the exact-dataset reality of CBIS-DDSM benchmarks (Tsochatzidis et al., 2021; Salama & Aly, 2021; Manzari et al., 2025; Rezk et al., 2026), and finally to the interpretability layer this study contributes through Grad-CAM (Tjoa & Guan, 2021; Balve & Hendrix, 2024; Ameen, 2025). This progression establishes both the precedent for the thesis's approach and the specific, realistic performance band its own results should be judged against: a model whose ≈76% accuracy and ≈91% sensitivity sit squarely within comparable CBIS-DDSM literature and reflect the deliberate trade-off of training for explainability alongside classification.

## Recommendations
1. **Foreground sensitivity/recall** as the primary evaluation metric, reported together with accuracy, AUC, and specificity, justified explicitly using Li & Hsu (2022) and Maier-Hein et al. (2024).
2. **Explicitly cite the accuracy–explainability trade-off literature** (Angelov et al., 2021; Ghassemi et al., 2021; Bell et al., 2022) in the Results/Discussion chapter when presenting the model's accuracy, to frame it as an expected consequence of training for explainability rather than as a shortfall.
3. **Benchmark primarily against CBIS-DDSM-specific, classification-only studies** (Tsochatzidis et al., 2021; Salama & Aly, 2021; Manzari et al., 2025; Rezk et al., 2026), and whenever a higher accuracy figure from another study is cited, state clearly whether it came from a different, easier dataset, additional preprocessing/segmentation, or a fusion/ensemble pipeline.
4. **Cite the real-world clinical sensitivity range for mammography screening** (55–91%; Shi et al., 2025) as a benchmark showing that the thesis's ≈91% sensitivity compares favorably even against human-radiologist-grade screening performance.
5. **Report per-class recall** (benign vs. malignant separately), since aggregate metrics can obscure poor malignant-class sensitivity.

## Caveats
- The very high accuracy/sensitivity figures reported in parts of the literature (Montaha et al., 2021 on preprocessed VGG16; Munien & Viriri, 2021 on histopathology; Pattanaik et al., 2022 with an added ELM classifier) came from either heavier preprocessing, a different imaging modality, or an additional classifier layered on top of the CNN — not from plain end-to-end classification on full CBIS-DDSM mammograms — and are **not directly comparable** to this thesis's setup.
- Some directly comparable CBIS-DDSM benchmarks (Asad et al., 2026) currently exist only as **preprints** and should be cited as such.
- Reported CBIS-DDSM numbers are sensitive to ROI cropping, image resizing, and train/test partitioning; the thesis should confirm the exact subset and split it uses when comparing against any cited figure.
- The accuracy–explainability trade-off literature (Section 2.4) is a general finding across ML domains, not a mammography-specific guarantee; the thesis should still aim to report the best achievable accuracy consistent with faithful Grad-CAM explanations, rather than treating the trade-off as a reason not to optimize accuracy at all.
